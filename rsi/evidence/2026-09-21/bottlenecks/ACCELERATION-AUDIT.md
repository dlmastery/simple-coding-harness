# Useful progress does not establish sustained acceleration

The five timing scenarios are independent synthetic alternatives. Doubling proposal speed changes 10 minutes to 9.5: 5% less time, or about 5.26% more rounds per unit time if every round is identical. Those percentages have different denominators. Halving the dominant evaluation stage changes total time to 5.5 minutes. An extra one-minute check more than erases the proposal saving. A costlier verifier makes the fifth scenario slower still.

These calculations assume serial stages, no overlapping work, no queueing, and unchanged result quality. They do not show that faster evaluation remains scientifically valid. Execution is zero only in this illustration. Real experimentation needs execution, failed attempts, retries, data preparation, and other costs.

The separate synthetic increments 4, 2, 1 give cumulative gains 4, 6, 7. The total rises while each additional gain shrinks. No time or resource denominator is attached to those arbitrary units, so they cannot describe a measured acceleration rate.

The copied local lineage tells a different, empirical story. Both paths retain MAE 109.807668 after generation one and 99.175924 after generation two. The cumulative reduction is 10.631744. The fixed path used 16.686200 recorded fit-process seconds; the improver-comparison path used 32.935724, including both proposed arms. The corresponding descriptive gains per recorded fit-process second are about 0.637158 and 0.322803. These denominators exclude author work and unknown inference cost, so neither is progress per total research resource.

Both proposed improver revisions were rejected. All active rows retain the same improver hash. The jump from zero gain to a positive second-round gain does not prove sustained acceleration: there are only two known-task rounds, different proposed model changes, no accepted revised improver, no independent matched task population, and incomplete total costs. The greater comparison cost is evidence of the work spent testing proposals here, not a general theorem that improver search is inefficient.

To support a future acceleration claim, establish all of the following with actual records:

- Identify the changed improver and show that its accepted revision governs later improvement work.
- Compare old and revised improvers from matched starts on comparable, independently evaluated tasks.
- Track per-round increments, cumulative gain, and elapsed time or a clearly defined total-resource denominator; include rejected proposals and failed jobs.
- Account for changing task difficulty, evaluation quality, hardware, parallelism, human input, and model access.
- Observe enough independently repeated generations to distinguish sustained rate change from a single successful proposal, noise, or task selection.

The current evidence does not satisfy those conditions. The verdict is **sustained acceleration not established**, while the useful task improvement and bounded rejection behavior remain supported. This is an evidence judgment, not a prediction that acceleration cannot occur.
